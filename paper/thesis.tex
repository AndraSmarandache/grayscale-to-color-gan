\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Colorization of grayscale images has applications across several domains: restoring
historical photographs, improving the interpretability of medical scans, and
post-processing footage captured in low-light conditions where color information
is unavailable or unreliable. Doing this manually is time-consuming and requires
expert knowledge about the original scene. Automated colorization removes this
bottleneck and makes it feasible to process large archives at scale.

The problem is fundamentally ill-posed: a single grayscale image is consistent with
many possible color assignments. A gray stone and a gray sky have the same luminance,
but they demand entirely different colors. Any successful approach must therefore go
beyond pixel-level reasoning and understand the semantic content of the image.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a grayscale image represented by its luminance channel $L \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$,
the goal is to predict the two chrominance channels $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$
that, when combined with $L$ in the CIE L*a*b* color space, produce a perceptually
plausible color image. The mapping $f: L \mapsto (a, b)$ is one-to-many, meaning
there is no unique correct output. The evaluation must therefore measure both
pixel-level accuracy and perceptual realism.

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A full GAN-based colorization system built around a ResNet-18 U-Net
          generator and a PatchGAN discriminator, trained on a 13,000-image
          COCO subset in the LAB color space.

    \item \textbf{Spatial Color Affinity Loss}: a novel loss that enforces local
          color consistency by penalizing color discontinuities between adjacent
          pixels that have similar luminance.

    \item \textbf{No Grey Loss}: a novel loss that discourages the generator from
          predicting desaturated (near-grey) output by penalizing low $ab$ magnitude.

    \item \textbf{Uncertainty-Aware Colorization}: an extension that adds a
          lightweight uncertainty head to the generator, enabling the model to
          output a per-pixel confidence map alongside the color prediction.
          High uncertainty identifies regions where the model cannot reliably
          determine the correct color, making the output directly useful for
          human-in-the-loop photograph restoration.

    \item An ablation study quantifying the individual and combined effect of each
          loss component on PSNR, SSIM, and FID.

    \item An adversarial robustness evaluation: the model is tested under FGSM and
          PGD perturbations applied to the input $L$ channel, and a version trained
          with adversarial examples is compared against the baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Early Approaches to Image Colorization}

Before deep learning, colorization relied either on user guidance or on propagating
color from reference images. Levin et al.\ \cite{levin2004colorization} introduced
a scribble-based approach in which the user marks a few regions with approximate
colors; the system then propagates these colors globally under the assumption that
spatially adjacent pixels with similar luminance should share similar colors. While
effective for simple images, this method requires manual interaction and does not
scale to large image collections.

Reference-based approaches \cite{welsh2002transferring} automatically transfer color
from a user-provided example image by matching grayscale luminance and local texture
descriptors. These methods produce plausible results when a good reference is
available, but they are sensitive to the choice of reference and often introduce
artifacts when the reference does not closely match the target.

Both families of methods share a fundamental limitation: they do not reason about
the semantic content of the image. A deep blue sky and a gray concrete surface can
have identical local texture descriptors yet require very different colors.

\section{Deep Learning for Colorization}

The first large-scale fully automatic colorization system based on deep learning was
proposed by Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2016colorful}. They framed colorization as a
classification problem over a quantized $ab$ color space and trained a convolutional
network with a rebalanced cross-entropy loss that up-weighted rare colors to prevent
the model from defaulting to desaturated output. The resulting system produces
vibrant, plausible colorizations and was the first to demonstrate that a network can
learn to associate semantic regions (sky, grass, skin) with appropriate colors without
any user input.

Larsson et al.\ \cite{larsson2016learning} showed that features learned for
image-level semantic classification transfer directly to colorization. They attached
a colorization head to a VGG network pretrained on ImageNet, demonstrating that
object-level representations are sufficient to drive color prediction without
task-specific supervision.

Iizuka et al.\ \cite{iizuka2016let} introduced a network that fuses global image
features (obtained from a classification branch) with local features from a
convolutional encoder. This fusion addresses a key weakness of purely local models:
global context is needed to correctly color, for example, a sky that appears in only
part of the image.

\section{Generative Adversarial Networks}

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \cite{goodfellow2014generative} consist of
two networks trained in competition: a generator $G$ that produces synthetic samples
and a discriminator $D$ that tries to distinguish them from real ones. The two
networks are trained with the minimax objective:

\begin{equation}
    \min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)]
    + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]
\end{equation}

In this formulation the generator must produce samples indistinguishable from real
data, which drives it toward perceptually realistic output — something that pure
regression losses like L1 cannot achieve.

For image-to-image translation tasks, Isola et al.\ \cite{isola2017image} introduced
the conditional GAN framework (pix2pix), in which both $G$ and $D$ are conditioned
on an input image. The generator receives the source image and produces the target;
the discriminator receives the source-target pair and must judge whether the target
is real or synthesized. The pix2pix discriminator uses a PatchGAN architecture that
classifies overlapping $N \times N$ patches of the image rather than the whole image,
which encourages the generator to produce locally consistent texture and sharp edges.

\section{Loss Functions in Image Synthesis}

The choice of loss function has a large effect on the quality and style of the
generated output. Regression losses (L1, L2) are simple and stable but produce
blurry predictions because they average over the space of plausible outputs. The
adversarial loss mitigates this by pushing the generator toward the manifold of
realistic images, but it can also introduce instability and mode collapse.

Johnson et al.\ \cite{johnson2016perceptual} introduced perceptual losses based
on feature activations from a pretrained VGG network. By comparing deep features
rather than raw pixels, perceptual losses capture style and semantic similarity
more effectively than L1. They have since become standard in image synthesis tasks.

Total Variation (TV) regularization penalizes spatial variation in the output,
producing smoother images and suppressing high-frequency artifacts. It is a classical
signal processing technique that has been adapted for deep image synthesis.

The loss functions in this thesis build on this foundation. The baseline uses
L1 + adversarial + TV + contrast. The two novel contributions (Spatial Color Affinity
and No Grey Loss) address specific failure modes observed in the baseline: isolated
color patches in uniform regions and desaturated output, respectively.

\section{Color Space}

Image colorization can be formulated in several color spaces. Working directly in RGB
couples luminance and chrominance information, which complicates the learning task.
We instead use the CIE L*a*b* (LAB) color space, which separates lightness $L^*$
from the two color-opponent channels $a^*$ (green--red axis) and $b^*$ (blue--yellow
axis).

This separation aligns naturally with the colorization problem: given a grayscale
image (which corresponds directly to the $L^*$ channel), the model only needs to
predict the $a^*$ and $b^*$ channels. The generator therefore receives a
single-channel input ($L$) and produces a two-channel output ($ab$).

Both channels are normalized before training. The $L$ channel (range $[0, 100]$) is
mapped to $[-1, 1]$ via $L/50 - 1$. The $ab$ channels (range approximately
$[-128, 127]$) are mapped to $[-1, 1]$ via division by $110$.

\section{Dataset Preparation}

We use the Microsoft COCO dataset \cite{lin2014microsoft} as our training corpus,
which provides a diverse collection of natural images across a wide range of scenes
and objects.

The raw COCO dataset contains a non-trivial fraction of grayscale images, which are
unsuitable for colorization training as they provide no ground-truth color
information. To address this, we applied an automated grayscale detection step: each
image is converted to RGB and the three channels are compared pixel-wise; images
where all channels are identical are removed. Because some images are
\textit{near-grayscale} (heavily desaturated or filtered), automated detection alone
is insufficient. A manual inspection pass was therefore performed on the resulting
subset to remove any remaining problematic images.

To guarantee a sufficient number of valid images after manual filtering, we extracted
a subset of 16,000 images from the full dataset using a fixed random seed (42) for
reproducibility. After the manual pass, approximately 13,000 color images remained.
These were split into training and validation sets using an 80/20 ratio, yielding
approximately 10,400 training images and 2,600 validation images.

\section{Model Architecture}

    \subsection{Generator: ResNet-18 U-Net}

    The generator follows a U-Net architecture \cite{ronneberger2015u} with a
    ResNet-18 encoder backbone \cite{he2016deep}. U-Net skip connections pass
    encoder feature maps directly to the corresponding decoder layers, preserving
    fine-grained spatial detail that would otherwise be lost through the bottleneck.
    This is important for colorization, where local texture and edge information
    must be retained in the predicted color output.

    ResNet-18 is used as the encoder because its residual connections enable
    effective gradient flow and because ImageNet-pretrained weights provide a strong
    initialization for feature extraction. Pretraining is critical given our
    relatively small dataset of 13,000 images: the encoder already recognizes shapes,
    textures, and objects relevant to color prediction without needing to learn these
    representations from scratch.

    Residual connections in ResNet address the vanishing gradient problem that
    limited very deep networks before their introduction. Each residual block
    computes $\mathcal{F}(x) + x$, where $\mathcal{F}$ is the learned residual
    mapping, so gradients can flow directly through the identity shortcut.

    Since ResNet-18 expects a three-channel RGB input, but our model receives a
    single $L$ channel, the first convolutional layer is adapted: a new
    \texttt{Conv2d(1, 64)} layer is created and its weights are initialized by
    averaging the three input-channel weights of the original pretrained layer.

    The decoder is built using \texttt{fastai}'s \texttt{DynamicUnet}, which
    automatically constructs the upsampling path with skip connections matched to
    the encoder. The final output is a two-channel $ab$ prediction in the range
    $[-1, 1]$ (Tanh activation).

    \subsection{Discriminator: PatchGAN}

    The discriminator is a PatchGAN \cite{isola2017image}, which classifies
    overlapping image patches as real or fake rather than producing a single scalar
    judgment for the entire image. This encourages the generator to produce locally
    consistent texture and sharp edges, since the discriminator penalizes unrealistic
    color at the patch level.

    The architecture consists of a sequence of strided convolutions that progressively
    increase the number of channels ($64 \to 128 \to 256 \to 512$) while reducing
    spatial resolution. The final layer produces a one-channel logit map without
    activation, and training uses \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss} on these raw logits.
    The conditional discriminator receives the concatenation of the $L$ channel and
    the $ab$ channels (real or generated) as a three-channel input, making it aware
    of the input luminance when judging color realism.

\section{Loss Functions}

    \subsection{Baseline Losses}

    The generator is trained with a combination of four baseline losses.

    \textbf{L1 Loss.} A pixel-wise L1 distance between the predicted $ab$ channels
    and the ground truth, weighted by $\lambda_{L1} = 100$. This provides the primary
    supervision signal and encourages overall color accuracy.

    \textbf{GAN Loss.} The standard adversarial loss \cite{goodfellow2014generative}
    using the PatchGAN discriminator with \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}. The generator
    is trained to fool the discriminator, which encourages it to produce perceptually
    realistic colors rather than just minimizing pixel error.

    \textbf{Total Variation Loss.} Penalizes large color differences between
    horizontally and vertically adjacent pixels, computed over the predicted $ab$
    channels. This reduces color artifacts and smooths transitions in uniform regions
    such as sky, snow, or plain surfaces.

    \textbf{Contrast Loss.} Encourages the generator to match the contrast level of
    the ground truth image. Contrast is measured as the standard deviation of the
    $L$ channel. This helps in scenes with extreme lighting conditions, such as night
    scenes or strongly lit outdoor images.

    \subsection{Spatial Color Affinity Loss}
    \label{sec:affinity}

    We introduce the Spatial Color Affinity Loss, which enforces local color
    consistency based on luminance similarity. The intuition is that adjacent pixels
    with similar luminance should also receive similar colors — for example, pixels
    in a uniformly lit sky region should not produce isolated color patches.

    For each pair of horizontally or vertically adjacent pixels $(i, j)$, we compute
    the luminance difference $|L_i - L_j|$. If this difference is below a threshold
    $\tau$, the pair is considered luminance-similar and contributes to the loss. The
    loss penalizes the mismatch between the color smoothness of the prediction and
    the color smoothness of the ground truth for those pairs:

    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{affinity}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}
        \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{N}}
        \mathbf{1}[|L_i - L_j| < \tau] \cdot
        \bigl| \|ab^{\text{pred}}_i - ab^{\text{pred}}_j\|_1
               - \|ab^{\text{gt}}_i - ab^{\text{gt}}_j\|_1 \bigr|
    \end{equation}

    where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of all adjacent pixel pairs (horizontal and
    vertical), and $\tau = 0.1$ in normalized $L$ space. The loss is computed
    separately for horizontal and vertical directions and summed.

    Unlike Total Variation, which unconditionally penalizes color variation, the
    affinity loss is \textit{selective}: it only penalizes pairs where luminance
    similarity suggests the colors should be consistent. This avoids suppressing
    legitimate color transitions at semantic boundaries.

    \subsection{No Grey Loss}
    \label{sec:nogrey}

    A well-known failure mode of colorization models trained with L1 loss is
    desaturated output: the model learns to predict near-zero $ab$ values because
    this minimizes the expected L1 error over the distribution of plausible colors.
    The adversarial loss partially mitigates this, but the problem persists,
    especially in regions with ambiguous color.

    We introduce the No Grey Loss, which directly penalizes low-magnitude $ab$
    predictions:

    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{nogrey}} =
        \frac{1}{B \cdot H \cdot W}
        \sum_{b,h,w} \max\bigl(0,\; \epsilon - \|ab^{\text{pred}}_{b,h,w}\|_2\bigr)
    \end{equation}

    where $\epsilon$ is a small saturation threshold (we use $\epsilon = 0.05$ in
    normalized $ab$ space). The loss contributes only for pixels where the predicted
    color is less saturated than $\epsilon$, providing a soft lower bound on color
    saturation without forcing all pixels to be strongly colored.

    \subsection{Uncertainty-Aware Colorization}
    \label{sec:uncertainty}

    Standard colorization models predict a single color per pixel, implicitly
    assuming the mapping from luminance to color is deterministic. In practice,
    colorization is ill-posed: a gray concrete wall, a gray stone, and a gray road
    all have similar luminance but could plausibly receive different colors.
    Predicting a single value forces the model to average over these possibilities,
    which contributes to the desaturated output characteristic of L1-trained models.

    We extend the generator with an \textit{uncertainty head}: a single $1 \times 1$
    convolutional layer that takes the predicted mean $\hat{ab}$ as input and
    produces a per-pixel log-variance $\log \sigma^2$ for each of the two ab
    channels. The generator therefore outputs both a color prediction and a
    confidence estimate without any change to the encoder-decoder backbone.

    The reconstruction loss is replaced with the Gaussian negative log-likelihood:

    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} = \frac{1}{2}
        \left( \log \sigma^2 + \frac{(ab^{\text{gt}} - \hat{ab})^2}{\sigma^2} \right)
        = \frac{1}{2} \left( \log \sigma + (ab^{\text{gt}} - \hat{ab})^2 e^{-\log \sigma^2} \right)
    \end{equation}

    This loss has two complementary terms. The reconstruction term
    $(ab^{\text{gt}} - \hat{ab})^2 e^{-\log \sigma^2}$ penalizes errors more
    heavily when the model claims high confidence (small $\sigma^2$). The
    regularization term $\log \sigma^2$ prevents the model from trivially avoiding
    reconstruction penalties by claiming everything is uncertain. Together they
    force the model to match the uncertainty to the actual difficulty of prediction:
    high uncertainty for ambiguous gray regions (walls, metal, concrete), low
    uncertainty for semantically unambiguous regions (sky, grass, skin).

    \textbf{Training strategy.} The uncertainty head is initialized with zero weights
    and a bias of $-2$, so $\log \sigma^2 \approx -2$ initially and the variance
    starts near $e^{-2} \approx 0.14$. Training proceeds in two phases. In Phase~1
    (5 epochs), the backbone is frozen and only the uncertainty head is trained, so
    the head learns to estimate uncertainty without disturbing the pretrained color
    predictions. In Phase~2 (15 epochs), all parameters are unfrozen for joint
    fine-tuning.

    The uncertainty map $\sigma(x, y) = \sqrt{\text{mean}_{c}(\sigma^2_c(x,y))}$
    is a direct output of the model at inference time, requiring no additional
    computation. It can be visualized as a heatmap over the colorized image to
    identify regions where the model is unsure — directly useful for
    human-in-the-loop restoration workflows where expert attention should focus
    on the most ambiguous areas.

    \subsection{Combined Generator Loss}

    The full generator loss is:

    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_G = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}} + \lambda_{L1}\,\mathcal{L}_{L1}
        + \lambda_{\text{TV}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{TV}}
        + \lambda_{\text{contrast}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrast}}
        + \lambda_{\text{affinity}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{affinity}}
        + \lambda_{\text{nogrey}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{nogrey}}
    \end{equation}

    Table~\ref{tab:weights} lists the default weights used in the full model.

    \begin{table}[H]
        \centering
        \caption{Loss weights used in the full model configuration.}
        \begin{tabular}{lcc}

\section{Training Procedure}

    Training proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, the generator is pretrained
    using only the L1 loss for 20 epochs with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$. This
    gives the generator a stable initialization before adversarial training begins,
    avoiding the instability that can occur when the discriminator overwhelms an
    untrained generator.

    In the second stage, the full GAN is trained for 20 additional epochs. Both the
    generator and discriminator use the Adam optimizer with learning rate
    $2 \times 10^{-4}$, $\beta_1 = 0.5$, and $\beta_2 = 0.999$. The low $\beta_1$
    is a standard recommendation for GAN training \cite{isola2017image}: it reduces
    the momentum term so the optimizer can respond more quickly to the rapidly
    changing gradient landscape. The discriminator is updated first on each
    iteration, followed by the generator. All experiments use a batch size of 16 on
    an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU.

\section{Experimental Setup}

All experiments are conducted on Google Colab using an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU. The
dataset consists of 13,000 color images from the COCO subset described in
Section~3.2, split 80/20 into training (10,400 images) and validation (2,600
images). All images are resized to $256 \times 256$ pixels. Training augmentation
is limited to random horizontal flipping.

Each experiment follows the two-stage training procedure described in Section~3.5:
20 epochs of generator pretraining with L1 loss only, followed by 20 epochs of full
adversarial training. The batch size is 16 for all runs. To keep experiments
reproducible, all runs share the same pretrained generator from Stage 1; only the
Stage 2 loss configuration changes between them.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate colorization quality using three complementary metrics.

\textbf{PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio)} measures pixel-level reconstruction
accuracy in decibels. Higher values indicate closer agreement between predicted and
ground-truth $ab$ channels. While straightforward to compute, PSNR does not always
correlate with perceptual quality.

\textbf{SSIM (Structural Similarity Index)} captures perceptual similarity by
comparing luminance, contrast, and structure between the predicted and ground-truth
images. Values range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect structural agreement.

\textbf{FID (Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance)} \cite{heusel2017gans} measures the
distributional distance between the feature representations of generated and real
images, computed using a pretrained Inception network. Lower FID indicates that the
colorized images are statistically closer to real color photographs. FID is the most
reliable indicator of perceptual realism among the three metrics.

\section{Adversarial Robustness}

To evaluate the robustness of the trained model, we test it under adversarial
perturbations generated by the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)
\cite{goodfellow2014explaining} and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)
\cite{madry2018towards}. Perturbations are applied to the input $L$ channel before
inference, simulating a scenario where the input grayscale image has been
imperceptibly modified.

FGSM constructs a perturbation in a single step:

\begin{equation}
    L^{\text{adv}} = L + \varepsilon \cdot \text{sign}(\nabla_L \mathcal{L}(f(L), ab))
\end{equation}

PGD iterates this process with multiple smaller steps and projects back to the
$\varepsilon$-ball after each step, producing stronger perturbations. We test
$\varepsilon \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05\}$ in normalized $L$ space.

We compare the baseline model against a version trained with adversarial examples
injected during training, measuring the degradation in PSNR and SSIM under
increasing perturbation magnitudes.

\section{Effect of Novel Loss Functions}

The Spatial Color Affinity Loss (Section~\ref{sec:affinity}) targets a specific
artifact that appears in the baseline: isolated blobs of color in regions that should
be uniformly colored (snow, sky, flat walls). The loss does not suppress all color
variation — only variation in regions where the luminance is flat. This selectivity
is important: a global smoothness penalty would reduce artifacts but would also
suppress legitimate color boundaries.

The No Grey Loss (Section~\ref{sec:nogrey}) addresses the tendency of L1-trained
models to predict desaturated output. By applying a soft penalty to low-saturation
pixels, it nudges the generator toward more vibrant predictions without changing
the behavior in well-saturated regions.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Dataset size.} The 13,000-image training set is small compared to the
hundreds of thousands of images used by state-of-the-art systems. This limits the
model's ability to generalize to rare object categories.

\textbf{Near-grayscale images.} Although obvious grayscale images were filtered out,
some heavily desaturated images may remain in the dataset. These provide weak
training signal and may contribute to the model's tendency toward desaturated output.

\textbf{No perceptual loss.} We did not include a VGG-based perceptual loss
\cite{johnson2016perceptual}. This is a known limitation: perceptual losses improve
sharpness and semantic coherence, especially in regions with fine texture.

\textbf{Fixed resolution.} The model is trained and evaluated at $256 \times 256$
pixels. Inference on higher-resolution images requires resizing, which may introduce
artifacts at object boundaries.

\section{Future Work}

Several directions for future work are worth pursuing. First, scaling the dataset
to several hundred thousand images would likely close the gap with state-of-the-art
systems. Second, incorporating a VGG perceptual loss would improve texture fidelity.
Third, the Spatial Color Affinity Loss could be extended to use learned edge maps
(from a pretrained edge detector) rather than the simple luminance-difference mask,
which would make it more robust to over-smoothing at legitimate color boundaries.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This thesis presented a GAN-based system for automatic grayscale image colorization.
The system uses a ResNet-18 U-Net generator and a PatchGAN discriminator operating
in the LAB color space, trained on a 13,000-image COCO subset. Two novel loss
functions were introduced: Spatial Color Affinity Loss, which enforces local color
consistency in uniform luminance regions, and No Grey Loss, which penalizes
desaturated predictions. An ablation study was conducted to isolate the contribution
of each loss component, and the model was evaluated for robustness under FGSM and
PGD adversarial perturbations. % TODO: add key numerical result once experiments are complete.
